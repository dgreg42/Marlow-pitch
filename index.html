The room

What we make, why it is built out of what it is built out of, and what it is supposed to feel like at six in the morning.

The premise

I have never been in a home gym that felt good to be in.

Not one. And I have been in a lot of them, in houses where every other room was thought about carefully by somebody who was paid well to think about it. Then you open one more door and it is a bench press, some weights and a kettlebell thrown in a room.

Stripped down, industrial, metal and plastic, lit like a parking garage. In the most considered house on the street it is the one room nobody considered. You can feel it the second you walk in, and it is why the door usually stays shut.

That is the whole idea. There is a room missing from these houses and we know how to make it.

Private gyms and wellness rooms, designed, supplied, installed and looked after. North Texas first, then New York and New England.

Materials

We build these out of natural materials so they do not feel like a gym.

Limewash plaster on the walls, because it moves through the day instead of sitting flat. Rift sawn white oak on the floor so the grain stays quiet underfoot. Honed limestone and terrazzo in the wet rooms, never polished, because polished stone and wet feet are a bad idea somebody has every year.

Leather, wool and linen where you sit down. Western red cedar in the sauna, for the smell and because it stays cool enough to lean on. Marble, iron, rubber, brushed stainless. Something green and alive in every room, which sounds like a small thing and is the difference between a space and a room.

And a scent, because a room has one whether anybody chose it or not. Cedar, leather, bergamot, vetiver.

Craft

Gym equipment is the only furniture in the house nobody expects to look at.

Everything else got chosen. The chairs, the lighting, the door handles, the stone on the island. Then the gym turns up in black powder coat and moulded rubber and goes in the basement behind a door, because that is what you do with something you would rather not see.

Make it worth looking at and the room moves. It comes up off the basement floor, sits next to the living space with glass instead of a door, and then it gets used differently, because you walk past it forty times a day instead of deciding to go down there. A room you would show somebody gets used. A room you apologise for does not.

So it gets drawn the way furniture gets drawn. A rack is a piece of furniture that happens to hold iron. Oak uprights sized to the wall it is going on rather than to a catalogue. Steel welded, ground and then blackened by hand, because powder coat chips and shows silver underneath and blackened steel just wears. Hardware countersunk so nothing catches a knuckle.

Most of the drawing time goes on transitions. Where the rubber platform meets the oak floor there is a brass strip set flush with both, instead of a moulding sitting proud of them. The floor vent is level with the boards. The plate post has a turned oak cap on it rather than a bare threaded rod. You are not supposed to notice any of that. You notice it by never catching your eye on anything.

It is also the part that cannot be ordered. Anything we can buy well we buy. The pieces that decide how the room feels, the rack, the bench, the wall the equipment hangs on, get drawn for the house and made for it.

Colour story

Nobody picked these colours. They are what the materials already are.

Each colour is measured off the material itself. Dark to light, this is the whole range a room gets built from, with something green growing in the middle of it.

The feeling

Training is hard. The room should be on your side.

You are about to do something difficult, on purpose, at six in the morning, and you have every reason to go back to bed. A room that is cold and loud and grey does not help with that. A room you actually want to be in does.

So the first hour is what we design for. Warm light on a plaster wall instead of a strip light overhead. Wood you can feel the grain of. Leather wrapped handles. Music where you want it and a screen that is part of the room rather than shouting at you the moment you open the door.

Nothing loud. No neon. Nothing that looks like equipment pretending to be furniture. The test we hold everything to is whether you want to stay in there a few minutes longer than you meant to.

Technology

The screen disappears when it is off.

On the training wall it is a mirror until you want it to be something else. In the corner where you sit afterward it holds a photograph. In the sauna, the steam room and over the plunge there is no screen at all, because no manufacturer will put a humidity rating on one and we would rather say so than find out.

The speakers are behind the plaster. Invisible drivers finished over in the dry rooms, from the makers who will sign off on limewash going over them. Sealed units in the ceiling where it is wet. In the heat, transducers that turn the cedar wall itself into the speaker. The amplifiers live in a cool closet down the hall and only the wire goes into the hot room.

Train

Brighter and cooler, the light your body reads as morning. Music up. The wall becomes a screen if you want one there.

Recover

Warm and low, closer to an hour after sunset. Music quiet, no screen, and the keypad showing where the sauna and the plunge have got to. Nothing else asks you for anything.

One keypad by the door does both, and so does the phone in your hand, and your voice if you want it. It runs inside whatever system the house already has rather than asking anyone to learn another app. The third scene is Off, and off is off.

The twenty minutes afterward are the part you miss when you travel.

Two halves

There are two halves to this. The work, and everything after it.

Health at home is not one room and the industry keeps selling it as one. There is the hour you put in, and there is the heat, the cold, the water and the sitting still that decides whether you can do it again on Thursday. Both of us have had a sauna and cold water within reach for years and it is the part we would not give up.

So we draw both from the first meeting. It is also the commercially serious half: growing faster, skewing older, carrying the bigger budget, and it is the one that has to be settled before the walls close because it wants water, drainage and power the house was never drawn for.

Performance

A platform that takes a dropped bar without the whole house hearing it. A rack in white oak and brushed steel rather than black powder coat. Leather wrapped handles. Whatever cardio somebody will really get on, and none of the rest.

Sixty to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

Restoration

Sauna, cold plunge and steam where the house will carry them. Cedar, honed limestone, linen, and a shower big enough to stand in without touching anything. Somewhere to sit afterward that is better than the bench you came off.

Forty to two hundred thousand.

The work

How a room gets drawn, specified and installed, from the conversation that finds out what somebody actually does to the amperage the steam room needs.

The brief

Most people ask for a gym and then describe something else.

Somebody says they want the full setup. Two conversations later it turns out they ride for forty minutes most mornings, they have a shoulder that has not been right since 2019, and the person actually in there five days a week is their wife.

That second version is the room we draw, and getting to it is most of the work. Their height sets the bar height and the shelf height. A shoulder that no longer goes overhead changes what lives on the wall. Six foot four means the ceiling matters more than the square footage.

Then every piece comes with a page saying why it is that one. The rack is forty two inches instead of the usual forty eight, because the door swings left and the wider one would have cost them the corner. It is not the rack we sell the most of. We write that down because we earn a margin on what we supply, and it is how anyone can tell whether a thing is in the room for their sake or for ours.

Electrical

A steam room requires 125 amps on its own.

That is the big generator. The small one still wants 38. A traditional sauna heater wants 30 to 50 on a circuit of its own. A commercial treadmill needs one it does not share with anything, including its neutral. The plunge needs a GFCI line and no extension cord, ever. Our read is that a full suite adds up to its own subpanel, though no code sets that threshold and we would have the electrician confirm it on the house.

Sauna, plunge and red light off one corridor. Each is its own line on the technology sheet.

Which means the electrical gets drawn before the framer arrives. Every room leaves the builder with a one page technology sheet: circuits and amperage, where the subpanel goes, floor boxes under the cardio so no cord crosses the floor, blocking in the wall for the display and the speakers, where the low voltage runs and where the rack lives. It goes in with the rest of the rough in. Anything missed at that moment costs far more after the drywall. Dallas has been on the 2023 electrical code since May 2025, and the park cities are a separate check every time.

White space

The white space is smaller than we assumed, and more specific.

The pieces all exist. Nobody has put them in one place: both rooms, one studio, our own crew, several makers, a written reason for every piece, and a care plan that covers the sauna and the plunge and not just the treadmill. Selling through the trade rather than a showroom is the sixth, and it is the one our own research supports least.

Every part of that exists somewhere, just never together. The two that matter most are the written reason and the care plan. One is a trust argument nobody else can make without changing their business. The other is the difference between what this company is worth at four times earnings and at seven.

  • Every firm in the category writes the same way. Sanctuary, bespoke, elevate, elegance, one roof, nothing left to chance, world class. We wrote our refusal list before we did this research and it turned out to be an accurate map of the category's vocabulary.
  • Nobody services the sauna or the plunge. Water chemistry, heaters, seals, drainage, cedar. Those systems are in the house, they degrade, and the owner cannot fix them. That is exactly where the repeat revenue sits, and the category treats the room as an add on.
  • Nobody has said out loud that they earn on what they specify. Which means every buyer in this market is quietly wondering about it and nobody has answered.

The systems that need looking after are the ones nobody else will service.

The two we miss are the same problem twice. At Year 5 there are about a hundred and forty seven room years of care plans running, which is seven hundred and sixty four thousand dollars, or seven point four percent of revenue. Getting to forty percent would take forty three thousand dollars a year per room and nobody pays that to have a sauna looked after. The Year 5 EBITDA margin is thirteen point eight percent against a fifteen percent bar. Both are held down by the same thing: sixty one thousand dollars of equipment moves through every room at a twenty eight percent margin, which inflates the revenue line and deflates every ratio measured against it.

So this gets judged on the wrong denominator twice, and the answer is not to argue with the denominator. It is to say plainly where that leaves the number.

What the plan says

Five and a half to seven times. The bottom of it sits inside the band. The top does not, and we do not think the top is supported. We would rather correct our own plan in front of you than have you find it.

What is left is the part that actually compounds. Every room handed over keeps paying while the family owns the house, and the installed base grows in a year when nothing new is sold. That is worth having. It is not worth two turns of multiple by Year 5, and anyone who tells you it is has not done the division.

The open question is not demand. The houses are being drawn for the room whether or not we build it. It is whether this becomes a contract business or stays a project business, and every decision in the plan is aimed at that one question.

The last two lines are balance sheet.

Working capital comes down to one line in the contract. On an average project the equipment costs us sixty one thousand four hundred dollars, and a fifty percent deposit on signature is sixty thousand nine hundred, which is five hundred dollars short of the order. So the deposit is fifty five percent, not fifty, and the equipment is paid for before it is ordered with five and a half thousand to spare. That holds only if a maker will open an account at all. Several will not for a dealer with no history, and prepayment plus freight ahead of the second draw is the one real exposure in the model. It is what the sixty thousand reserve is for. From Year 3 what the balance sheet carries is payroll across a two month build, not equipment.

Whether this needs a second round: on these numbers, no. Year 3 pays for itself and Year 4 pays the hole back. A second round would mean the Northeast moving earlier than Year 2 or the install standard needing a third crew sooner than the table carries, and both of those are decisions we would bring to you rather than discover.

Still ours to settle, and we would rather say so than put a number on a page we have not earned: the structure, the percentage, and what a return looks like. The amount and what it does are above. The terms are a conversation.

None of this is hard. It has just never been anybody’s whole job.

The plan

What year one is for, the questions we would ask us, and the two people doing it.

The stages

The first job is to make something worth photographing.

A designer writes a specialist into a plan set eighteen months before anybody moves in, and she does it because she has seen the work. We have taste and a point of view and nothing yet to point at, so everything in year one exists to fix that.

  1. Now to spring 2027

    Settle the name. Sign dealer agreements across strength, cardio, sauna and plunge, never one maker. Make the first piece of furniture so there is something a designer can put her hand on. Build the trade kit and the one page rough in sheet a builder drops straight into a plan set, which costs nothing and puts us in the drawings before anybody has hired us.

  2. 2027

    Two flagship rooms through our own networks, one of each, priced to win, with the right to photograph them written into the contract. A staged show project with a spec builder, which stands in for the showroom we do not want yet. Photograph both to magazine standard and go at the design press.

  3. 2028

    Grow the trade program toward twenty five active Texas accounts. Sign audio visual integrators on a referral, which nobody in this category has done and which puts us inside houses that are already being wired. Publish one finished room's full spec, every maker and the reason for each piece.

  4. Year 3 and after

    Turn the pieces we have made into a short catalogue. Open the commercial line into hotels, clubs and schools. Look again at the Northeast, either through a second crew or through the Connecticut firm currently for sale.

Risks

What we would ask us about.

Anybody looking at this seriously is going to find the following, so here it is from us first.

  • Two people and nothing finished. Every claim here is a point of view until the first two rooms exist. That is the whole reason year one is built around getting them made and photographed.
  • Those two rooms are sold at cost. Ninety seven thousand dollars of margin given up on purpose, and it is inside the Year 1 line above rather than hidden outside the table.
  • The Year 5 numbers assume a third install crew we have not priced. Our own review of the plan flagged it and the tables do not carry it. Our own figure for a crew is two hundred and fifty to three hundred and fifty thousand a year, which would take Year 5 EBITDA nearer $1.1M and the valuation band down with it. Treat $1.42M as the top of the range.
  • Nothing is signed with a maker yet. The whole model rests on a 25 to 35 percent dealer margin and we hold none of it today. Premium makers commonly want a showroom, a territory volume commitment or a dealer history before they grant it, and we have none of the three. If Year 1 lands at fifteen percent instead, this plan does not work at this raise.
  • The multiple is bought by the Northeast and by hiring, not by the care plan. We used to say the care plan was the valuation. The arithmetic says it is seven point four percent of Year 5 revenue, so if either of the other two slips there is nothing underneath to catch it.
  • Six projects in Year 1 means every client is seventeen percent of the year. Concentration above ten percent is on the same list that prices the multiple, and the trade channel concentrates it further, because two or three designers could be most of the book before the fourth arrives.
  • The ramp already assumes the Northeast from Year 2, and our own stage list does not reach it until Year 3. That is a real disagreement inside our plan and we have not resolved it. Until the second market opens this is a bet on North Texas building and renovating, and if that slows in 2028 the ramp slows with it.
  • The install is the promise. The moment it is handed to whoever is free that week, the thing we are selling stops being true. Crews have to grow before markets do, which is the argument for Texas first.
  • Somebody assembles this before we do. The New York firm already has most of the gym side. If it added wellness and turned to the trade it would be in front of us, and it has four showrooms and we have none.

It is still on at ten at night, which is the whole point.

Us

Dan draws it. Dave sells it and gets it built.

Dan has spent twenty years as a creative director on brands you would recognise, with an Emmy from one of them. He does the drawings, the materials, the light and everything you can see. What he actually cares about is why some rooms feel good to stand in and most do not.

Dave spent his in Texas law and wealth management, which means twenty years in rooms with the people who buy these houses. He does the makers, the trade, the crew and the money.

They both train seriously and have for a long time. Dave rides. Dan does CrossFit and is a purple belt in jiu-jitsu. Between them they have used a lot of rooms, and neither of them has been in one like the one they are describing, which is most of why they are doing it.

Let us build the first one for you.

We would rather take money from somebody who would actually want one of these. The first room being yours is the cheapest proof either of us will ever get, and it is the one thing in this document that beats reading it.
