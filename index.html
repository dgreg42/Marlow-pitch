I have never been in a home gym that felt good to be in.

Not one. And I have been in a lot of them, in houses where every other room was thought about carefully by somebody who was paid well to think about it. Then you open one more door and it is a bench press, some weights and a kettlebell thrown in a room.

Stripped down, industrial, metal and plastic, lit like a parking garage. In the most considered house on the street it is the one room nobody considered. You can feel it the second you walk in, and it is why the door usually stays shut.

That is the whole idea. There is a room missing from these houses and we know how to make it.

Private gyms and wellness rooms, designed, supplied, installed and looked after. North Texas first, then New York and New England.

We build these out of natural materials so they do not feel like a gym.

Limewash plaster on the walls, because it moves through the day instead of sitting flat. Rift sawn white oak on the floor so the grain stays quiet underfoot. Honed limestone and terrazzo in the wet rooms, never polished, because polished stone and wet feet are a bad idea somebody has every year.

Leather, wool and linen where you sit down. Western red cedar in the sauna, for the smell and because it stays cool enough to lean on. Marble, iron, rubber, brushed stainless. Something green and alive in every room, which sounds like a small thing and is the difference between a space and a room.

And a scent, because a room has one whether anybody chose it or not. Cedar, leather, bergamot, vetiver.

Training is hard. The room should be on your side.

You are about to do something difficult, on purpose, at six in the morning, and you have every reason to go back to bed. A room that is cold and loud and grey does not help with that. A room you actually want to be in does.

So the first hour is what we design for. Warm light on a plaster wall instead of a strip light overhead. Wood you can feel the grain of. Leather wrapped handles. Music where you want it and a screen that is part of the room rather than shouting at you the moment you open the door.

Nothing loud. No neon. Nothing that looks like equipment pretending to be furniture. The test we hold everything to is whether you want to stay in there a few minutes longer than you meant to.

The twenty minutes afterward are the part you miss when you travel.

There are two halves to this. The work, and everything after it.

Health at home is not one room and the industry keeps selling it as one. There is the hour you put in, and there is the heat, the cold, the water and the sitting still that decides whether you can do it again on Thursday. Both of us have had a sauna and cold water within reach for years and it is the part we would not give up.

So we draw both from the first meeting. It is also the commercially serious half: growing faster, skewing older, carrying the bigger budget, and it is the one that has to be settled before the walls close because it wants water, drainage and power the house was never drawn for.

Performance

A platform that takes a dropped bar without the whole house hearing it. A rack in white oak and brushed steel rather than black powder coat. Leather wrapped handles. Whatever cardio somebody will really get on, and none of the rest.

Sixty to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

Restoration

Sauna, cold plunge and steam where the house will carry them. Cedar, honed limestone, linen, and a shower big enough to stand in without touching anything. Somewhere to sit afterward that is better than the bench you came off.

Nobody has to be convinced the room belongs in the house any more. That argument is over and we did not have to win it. The open question is who builds it.

Most people ask for a gym and then describe something else.

Somebody says they want the full setup. Two conversations later it turns out they ride for forty minutes most mornings, they have a shoulder that has not been right since 2019, and the person actually in there five days a week is their wife.

That second version is the room we draw, and getting to it is most of the work. Their height sets the bar height and the shelf height. A shoulder that no longer goes overhead changes what lives on the wall. Six foot four means the ceiling matters more than the square footage.

Then every piece comes with a page saying why it is that one. The rack is forty two inches instead of the usual forty eight, because the door swings left and the wider one would have cost them the corner. It is not the rack we sell the most of. We write that down because we earn a margin on what we supply, and it is how anyone can tell whether a thing is in the room for their sake or for ours.

We went looking for who already does this, and two of our assumptions were wrong.

We read every firm in the country we could find doing high end home gyms and wellness rooms. Saying what we found is cheaper than having somebody find it for us.

There is no national platform in this category. It is a long tail of small regional firms, mostly owner operated, plus manufacturer run design programs that can only sell one brand. The only public financial data point we could find is a three year old firm in Connecticut with $1.28M of revenue asking $795,000 for the whole business. That is the scale of the field.

Private equity has consolidated audio visual integration and pool service in the last two years and has not touched this. The adjacent categories have platforms and this one does not.

The white space is smaller than we assumed, and more specific.

The pieces all exist. Nobody has put them in one place: both rooms, one studio, our own crew, several makers, a written reason for every piece, and a care plan that covers the sauna and the plunge and not just the treadmill. Selling through the trade rather than a showroom is the sixth, and it is the one our own research supports least.

Every part of that exists somewhere, just never together. The two that matter most are the written reason and the care plan. One is a trust argument nobody else can make without changing their business. The other is the difference between what this company is worth at four times earnings and at seven.

  • Every firm in the category writes the same way. Sanctuary, bespoke, elevate, elegance, one roof, nothing left to chance, world class. We wrote our refusal list before we did this research and it turned out to be an accurate map of the category's vocabulary.
  • Nobody services the sauna or the plunge. Water chemistry, heaters, seals, drainage, cedar. Those systems are in the house, they degrade, and the owner cannot fix them. That is exactly where the repeat revenue sits, and the category treats the room as an add on.
  • Nobody has said out loud that they earn on what they specify. Which means every buyer in this market is quietly wondering about it and nobody has answered.

Four ways one room pays, and the last one never stops.

The playbook is the high end audio visual integrator of twenty years ago. Sell to the trade, get written into the plans early, and look after the house for as long as the family owns it.

The care plan is the valuation.

Everything above is a good small business. Whether it is worth four times its earnings or seven comes down to one variable: how much of the revenue is recurring.

Year 5 in the conservative case is $1.42M of EBITDA. That sits in the band that prices at four to six times, so $5.7M to $8.5M. The plan's conservative valuation of $7.8M to $9.9M is at the top of that band and above it, which works out at five and a half to seven times, and it is only reachable if three things are true.

  • The care plan attaches at a high rate from the very first handover, so recurring revenue is a real share of the total.
  • The Northeast opens on schedule in Year 2, which the conservative case already assumes. A model proven in two markets prices around two points higher than one proven in a single market, so a slip there costs more than the revenue it delays.
  • There is a layer of people under the two of us by Year 4, because owner dependent businesses are discounted and everybody knows it.

That is the investment case stated honestly. The open question is not demand, which the figures earlier in this document already speak to, and the houses are drawn for the room whether or not we build it. It is whether this becomes a contract business or stays a project business, and every decision in the plan is aimed at that one question.

What we would ask us about.

Anybody looking at this seriously is going to find the following, so here it is from us first.

  • Two people and nothing finished. Every claim here is a point of view until the first two rooms exist. That is the whole reason year one is built around getting them made and photographed.
  • Those two rooms are priced to win. They will carry less margin than the model says and that cost is not in the Year 5 table. It is a marketing spend we have chosen to take as lower margin instead.
  • The Year 5 numbers assume a third install crew we have not priced. The plan calls for it and the summary tables do not carry it, so treat the EBITDA line as the top of the range rather than the middle.
  • The valuation depends on the care plan, not on the rooms. If the attach rate is poor this is a four times business and the plan's enterprise value is wrong by several million dollars.
  • One metro and one cycle. Until the Northeast opens this is a bet on North Texas building and renovating. If that slows in 2028 the ramp slows with it.
  • The install is the promise. The moment it is handed to whoever is free that week, the thing we are selling stops being true. Crews have to grow before markets do, which is the argument for Texas first.
  • Somebody assembles this before we do. The New York firm already has most of the gym side. If it added wellness and turned to the trade it would be in front of us, and it has four showrooms and we have none.

They both train seriously and have for a long time. Dave rides. Dan does CrossFit and is a purple belt in jiu-jitsu. Between them they have used a lot of rooms, and neither of them has been in one like the one they are describing, which is most of why they are doing it.
