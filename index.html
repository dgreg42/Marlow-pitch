Marlow Bespoke private gyms and wellness rooms · North Texas

A part of your home you'll make time for.

A good workout. A long stretch. A few quiet minutes in the sauna.

It starts with what you love doing.

Maybe you've trained for years and know exactly what you want. Maybe you're making room for something you've been meaning to come back to.

We're interested in both.

What do you look forward to? What would you do more often if it were easier? Who else will use the space?

The room, in use

Somewhere to work hard. Somewhere to let go.

There's a particular pleasure in having everything close by. Finish a session and walk into the sauna. Stretch while your partner trains. Sit for a while before heading back into the house.

We think about those moments together. How you move through the room. Where you put your towel. The light when you arrive. Where you'll want to sit afterward.

It should feel good the whole time you are in it.

What belongs

What belongs in your room?

Strength and movement

Room to lift, stretch and move freely. Equipment chosen for the way you train, with the space around it given just as much thought.

Heat

A sauna or a steam room, sized for the way you'll actually use it. We work with your builder to establish what's possible before settling the design.

Cold

A plunge you'll get into more than once. The temperature you want, held there, and a floor and a drain that forgive the water.

A place to pause

A comfortable seat. Natural light where we can find it. Somewhere to have a drink of water and take your time.

Space for both of you

Different routines can share a room comfortably. We consider what each person needs, including the moments when one of you wants a little privacy.

Materials

It should feel at home here.

You've put thought into the rest of your house. This room deserves the same attention.

Oak, limestone, plaster, cedar and leather bring warmth and texture to spaces that are often treated as purely practical. We consider how they feel against your skin, how they hold the light, and how they'll look after years of use.

Some pieces will be made for your space. Others will come from makers whose work we believe in. Everything is chosen to work well together.

The details you live with.

A floor that feels good under bare feet. A bench with a comfortable edge. A handle you enjoy picking up. Storage that makes it easy to put things away.

These are small decisions, but you meet them every time you use the room.

How it works

From the first conversation to the finished room.

  1. We get to know you

    We talk about your routines, your house and what you'd like to make possible. You can arrive with a clear idea or a handful of things you're drawn to.

  2. Then we design it

    You'll see the layout, materials and equipment together, with a clear proposal and price. We explain our recommendations and work through the choices with you.

  3. We work with the people building your home

    Your designer and builder are part of the process. We coordinate the requirements early so the room fits naturally into their plans.

  4. We bring it together

    We coordinate supply and installation, then walk you through the finished space so you feel comfortable using everything in it.

Care

We'll help you keep it that way.

Once the room is yours, there are practical things to look after: equipment, heaters, filters and water systems.

Our care plans are designed around what's in your room. We'll agree on the schedule and scope with you, so you know who to call and what's being taken care of.

Marlow Investor overview · 2026

A considered room. A business built around it.

Marlow is being developed to handle that work as one studio.

People investing in their homes are making space for training, recovery and time to themselves. Bringing those spaces together requires decisions across design, equipment, building services and ongoing care.

We'll begin in North Texas, working with homeowners, designers and builders to create private gyms and wellness rooms. Revenue will come from design, product supply, installation and ongoing care.

Our first job is to complete two rooms that demonstrate the idea: spaces people enjoy using, built to a standard designers will recommend.

What we believe

A private gym can deserve the attention the rest of the house gets.

The same thought that goes into the kitchen, the living room or the garden.

The opportunity is to bring that attention to the whole experience, from the first drawing to the way the room is looked after years later.

What we need to prove

Three things the first projects have to establish.

  • That clients will pay for the complete offer. Design, supply, installation and care bought together rather than separately.

The gap

Nobody has put the pieces in one place.

The pieces all exist. Nobody has put them in one place: both rooms, one studio, our own crew, a rack from one company and a sauna from another, a written reason for every piece, and a care plan that covers the sauna and the plunge, not just the treadmill. Selling through the trade rather than a showroom is the sixth, and it is the one our own research supports least.

The two that matter most are the written reason and the care plan. One is a trust argument nobody else can make without changing their business. The other is the difference between what this company is worth at four times earnings and at seven.

  • Every firm in the category writes the same way. Sanctuary, elevate, elegance, one roof, world class. We wrote our refusal list before this research and it turned out to be an accurate map of the category's vocabulary.
  • Nobody services the sauna or the plunge. Water chemistry, heaters, seals, drainage, cedar. Those systems degrade and the owner cannot fix them. That is exactly where the repeat revenue sits.
  • Nobody has said out loud that they earn on what they specify. Every buyer in this market is quietly wondering, and nobody has answered.

The systems that need looking after are the ones nobody else will service.

Electrical

A steam room requires 125 amps on its own.

That is the big generator. The small one still wants 38. A traditional sauna heater wants 30 to 50 on a circuit of its own. A commercial treadmill needs one it does not share with anything, including its neutral. The plunge needs a GFCI line and no extension cord, ever. Our read is that a full suite adds up to its own subpanel, though no code sets that threshold and we would have the electrician confirm it on the house.

Sauna, plunge and red light off one corridor. Each is its own line on the technology sheet.

So the electrical gets drawn before the framer arrives. Every room leaves the builder with a one page technology sheet: circuits and amperage, where the subpanel goes, floor boxes under the cardio, blocking in the wall for the display and the speakers, where the low voltage runs. Anything missed at that moment costs far more after the drywall. Dallas has been on the 2023 electrical code since May 2025, and the park cities are a separate check every time.

Where we stand

Five things we decided before we started.

  • More than one brand. We will hold agreements with several manufacturers rather than one, so the rack can come from one and the sauna from another. Nobody has to take a package. None of those agreements are signed yet.
  • We make money on the equipment we sell. That goes in writing beside every piece, with the reason it is in the room, so an owner can tell whether it is there for them or for us.
  • Both rooms, one studio. The gym and the wellness room, one crew, and one care plan covering both.
  • We sell through designers and builders. They are drawing the house a year before anybody thinks about equipment, and that is when this gets decided.
  • Lighter and warmer than most gyms. The room has to sit beside the interior designer's work without fighting it.
Craft

Gym equipment is the only furniture in the house nobody expects to look at.

Everything else in the house got chosen. The chairs, the lighting, the door handles, the stone on the island. Then the gym turns up in black powder coat and goes in the basement behind a door.

Make it worth looking at and the room moves upstairs, beside the living space behind glass. Then it gets used, because you walk past it forty times a day instead of deciding to go down there.

So a rack gets drawn the way furniture gets drawn. Oak uprights sized to the wall rather than to a catalogue. Steel welded, ground and blackened by hand, because powder coat chips and blackened steel just wears. Hardware countersunk so nothing catches a knuckle.

Most of the drawing time goes on transitions. Every detail below is one, and you are not supposed to notice any of them.

Anything we can buy well, we buy. The pieces that decide how the room feels, the rack, the bench, the wall the equipment hangs on, get drawn for the house and made for it.

Risks

What we would ask us about.

Anybody looking at this seriously will find the following, so here it is from us first.

  • Two people and nothing finished. Every claim here is a point of view until the first two rooms exist. That is why year one is built around getting them made and photographed.
  • Those two rooms are sold at cost. $87,700 of margin given up on purpose, inside the Year 1 line rather than hidden outside the table.
  • The gross profit on a room does not cost our own time. The $46,920 counts the whole design fee as margin. Our drawing and project hours sit in overhead as founder pay, not against the room.
  • The Year 5 numbers assume a third install crew we have not priced. Our own figure for a crew is two hundred and fifty to three hundred and fifty thousand a year, which would take Year 5 EBITDA nearer $1.1M and the valuation band down with it. Treat $1.42M as the top of the range.
  • Nothing is signed with a maker yet. The whole model rests on a 25 to 35 percent dealer margin and we hold none of it today. Makers commonly want a showroom, a territory commitment or a dealer history first, and we have none of the three. If Year 1 lands at fifteen percent instead, this plan does not work at this raise.
  • The multiple is bought by the Northeast and by hiring, not by the care plan. We used to say the care plan was the valuation. It is seven point four percent of Year 5 revenue, so if either of the other two slips there is nothing underneath to catch it.
  • Six projects in Year 1 means every client is seventeen percent of the year. Any one client above ten percent of revenue is on the same list that prices the multiple, and the selling through designers concentrates it further.
  • The growth table assumes the Northeast from Year 2 and our own stage list does not reach it until Year 3. That is a real disagreement inside our plan and we have not resolved it. Until the second market opens this is a bet on North Texas building, and if that slows in 2028 the plan slows with it.
  • The install is the promise. The moment it is handed to whoever is free that week, the thing we are selling stops being true. Crews have to grow before markets do, which is the argument for Texas first.
  • Somebody assembles this before we do. The New York firm already has most of the gym side. If it added wellness and turned to the trade it would be in front of us, and it has four showrooms and we have none.
